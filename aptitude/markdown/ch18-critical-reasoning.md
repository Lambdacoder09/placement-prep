# Chapter 18 — Critical Reasoning

*Find the gap between the evidence and the claim.*

## What you need to know

**WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW**

**Every argument has three parts.**
- **Evidence** — the facts given.
- **Conclusion** — the claim being pushed. Look for: so, hence, therefore, thus, clearly,
  this shows, we must.
- **Gap** — the unstated jump from evidence to conclusion. Every question in this chapter
  attacks the gap.

**The seven question types and the one test for each.**

#table(columns: (auto, 1fr),
  [**Type**], [**Test**],
  [Statement–Assumption],
  [Is it **needed**? Negate it. If the argument collapses, it is an assumption.],
  [Statement–Conclusion],
  [Can it be got from the given words **alone**? No outside knowledge, no guessing.],
  [Statement–Inference],
  [Is it **probably true** given the facts, without over-reaching beyond the data set?],
  [Statement–Argument],
  [Strong = relevant + substantial + tied to the exact question. Weak otherwise.],
  [Course of Action],
  [Three filters: does it hit the **cause**, is it **practical**, is it inside the actor's **power**?],
  [Cause and Effect],
  [Which event came first and could stand alone? That one is the cause.],
  [Strengthen / Weaken],
  [Find the gap. Strengthen = close it or rule out a rival cause. Weaken = supply a rival cause or a counter-case.],
)

**The negation test, in three steps.**
+ Write the candidate assumption.
+ Flip it to its opposite ("some" $->$ "none", "will" $->$ "will not").
+ Re-read the statement. If the statement now makes no sense, the candidate **is** an
  assumption. If the statement still stands, it is not.

**Assumption versus inference.**
#table(columns: (auto, 1fr, 1fr),
  [], [**Assumption**], [**Inference**],
  [Where it sits], [**Before** the statement], [**After** the statement],
  [Status],        [Taken for granted, unsaid], [Drawn out, follows],
  [Question it answers], [Why does this make sense?], [What else must be true now?],
)

**Strong argument checklist.** An argument is **weak** if it is any of these:
vague, trivial, a single personal example, a re-statement of the question,
a pure appeal to emotion, based on a false comparison, or about a side issue.

**Course of action — reject if:** it is extreme (ban / shut down / expel / shift everyone),
it costs far more than the cheaper fix that is also on offer, it is outside the actor's
power, or it treats a symptom while ignoring a named cause.

**Cause and effect — the four labels.**
+ I is the cause and II is its effect.
+ II is the cause and I is its effect.
+ Both are effects of one common cause.
+ Both are independent causes.

**Percent versus absolute.** Growth rate $\times$ base $=$ the actual increase.
A 40% rise on a base of 120 adds 48. A 10% rise on a base of 900 adds 90.
The smaller rate can add much more.

**Before-and-after averages.** An average can rise with nobody improving, if the low
members were removed from the group. Always ask: **did the group itself change?**

## Warm-up

### Warm-up

**Example 1**

Split into evidence and conclusion: "Sales fell 20% right after the price rise.
So the price rise caused the fall."

**Solution**

The word "So" marks the conclusion.
Evidence: sales fell 20% after the price rise.
Conclusion: the price rise caused the fall.
Gap: something else in that period could have cut sales.

**➜ Answer: Evidence = sales fell after the price rise. Conclusion = the price rise caused it.**

**Example 2**

Advertisement: "Buy the Shakti helmet — it keeps you safe."
Is "people want to be safe" an assumption?

**Solution**

Negate it: "People do not want to be safe."
Then a helmet that keeps you safe is not a selling point, and the advertisement is
pointless. The argument collapses.

**➜ Answer: Yes, it is an assumption.**

**Example 3**

Statement: "The school has started evening classes for weak students."
Label each as assumption or inference:
(i) Some students in the school are weak. (ii) Weak students may improve.

**Solution**

(i) sits **before** the decision. If no student were weak, starting the classes would make
no sense. That is taken for granted — an assumption.
(ii) sits **after** the decision. It is a likely result, drawn out of the statement.

**➜ Answer: (i) assumption, (ii) inference.**

**Example 4**

Argument: "City X put speed cameras on Ring Road. Accidents fell 30%.
So cameras cut accidents." Which weakens it?
(a) The cameras were costly. (b) Ring Road was shut for repairs for half the period.

**Solution**

(a) is about money, not about whether cameras cut accidents. Irrelevant.
(b) gives a rival cause: fewer cars on the road means fewer accidents, cameras or not.

**➜ Answer: (b).**

**Example 5**

Same argument. Which strengthens it?
(a) Traffic volume on Ring Road stayed the same through the period.
(b) The city also bought new ambulances.

**Solution**

(a) shuts down the main rival cause, "there were simply fewer cars". That leaves the
cameras standing.
(b) is about treating injuries, not preventing crashes.

**➜ Answer: (a).**

**Example 6**

Problem: "Many students reach school late because they miss the single bus trip."
Which course of action follows?
(a) Run a second bus trip 30 minutes later. (b) Expel students who come late.

**Solution**

(a) hits the named cause (one trip only), costs little, and is inside the school's power.
(b) punishes the student for a problem the school created. Extreme, and it does not fix
the cause.

**➜ Answer: (a) only.**

**Example 7**

Event I: The state cut bus fares in July.
Event II: Bus ridership rose 25% in August. Label the pair.

**Solution**

I happened first, and a fare cut can happen on its own for policy reasons.
Cheaper travel pulls riders in. The order and the mechanism both run I $->$ II.

**➜ Answer: I is the cause and II is its effect.**

**Example 8**

Question: "Should coding tests be compulsory for all engineering students?"
Which argument is strong?
(a) Yes — most campus recruiters screen on coding, so the test matches what students
will actually face. (b) No — tests are hard.

**Solution**

(a) is relevant and substantial: it links the rule to a real outcome for the student.
(b) is vague and trivial. Every test is hard; that says nothing about this one.

**➜ Answer: (a) is strong; (b) is weak.**

## Tier 1 — Service companies

### Tier 1 — Service · TCS NQT, Accenture, Infosys, Wipro, Capgemini

**Example 9** `TCS NQT pattern`

**Statement:** A bank notice says, "Customers can now update their address through our
mobile app." \
**Assumptions:** I. Some customers have a smartphone with the app installed.
  II. No customer will ever visit a branch again.

**Solution**

I — negate it: "No customer has a smartphone with the app."
Then the notice reaches nobody and is useless. The notice collapses. I is an assumption.

II — negate it: "Some customers will still visit a branch."
The notice is still perfectly sensible; it offers an extra channel, not the only one.
The notice survives. II is not an assumption.

**➜ Answer: Only I is an assumption.**

---
⚠️ **TRAP**

**Extreme words kill an assumption.** "No customer will ever", "all", "only", "never",
"always" — an assumption has to be the **minimum** the argument needs.
The argument in Example 9 needs **some** app users, not **every** customer.
If an option is stronger than the argument needs, it is not an assumption.

**Example 10** `Accenture pattern`

**Statement:** In the last term, 60 of the 200 students of Class 10 scored above 80%. \
**Conclusions:** I. 140 students scored 80% or below.   II. The class average was
above 80%.

**Solution**

I: total students $= 200$. Students above 80% $= 60$.
Remaining $= 200 - 60 = 140$.
Every one of those 140 is "not above 80%", i.e. 80% or below. I follows.

II: 60 students scored high; the other 140 could all have scored 30%.
Rough check with those numbers: average $\approx (60 \times 85 + 140 \times 30) / 200
= (5100 + 4200) \div 200 = 9300 \div 200 = 46.5$%.
So the class average need not be above 80%. II does not follow.

**➜ Answer: Only I follows.**

**Example 11** `Infosys pattern`

**Statement:** The landfill serving a city will be full in six months. \
**Courses of action:** I. The city should start separating wet and dry waste at the
household level.   II. The city should stop collecting waste.

**Solution**

I — three filters. Cause: separated dry waste can be recycled, so less goes to the
landfill. Hits the cause. Practical: yes, many cities do it. Power: waste collection is
the city's own job. I follows.

II — stopping collection does not reduce waste; it moves it to the streets. It makes the
problem worse and is outside any sane duty of the city. II does not follow.

**➜ Answer: Only I follows.**

**Example 12** `Wipro pattern`

**Question:** Should voting be made compulsory? \
**Arguments:** I. Yes — when only a third of voters turn out, the winning side may
represent a small slice of the people.   II. No — many people are busy on polling
day.

**Solution**

I: it names a real, sizeable defect (a government chosen by a minority) and ties it
directly to the proposal. Relevant and substantial. Strong.

II: polling day is a declared holiday in most places, and "busy" is vague — no size, no
group, no number. It is also an argument against **any** voting arrangement, not this one.
Weak.

**➜ Answer: Only argument I is strong.**

**Example 13** `Capgemini pattern`

**Statement I:** The municipal body doubled parking fees in the central market in March. \
**Statement II:** Shops in the central market reported 15% lower footfall in April.

**Solution**

Order: March comes before April, so II cannot have caused I.
Mechanism: a higher parking fee raises the cost of a shopping trip, so fewer people
drive in. That runs I $->$ II.
Independence: the fee change was a decision of the municipal body; it did not need the
footfall drop to happen.

**➜ Answer: I is the cause and II is its effect.**

---
💡 **SHORTCUT**

**The cause-effect 3-question drill.** Run the pair through these in order and stop at the
first one that decides it.
+ **Which came first?** The later event can never be the cause. March before April settles
  Example 13 in one look.
+ **Can one happen on its own?** A policy decision, a rain spell, a rule change — these do
  not wait for the other event. That one is the cause.
+ **If neither of the above works**, ask whether a third thing (a festival, a new law, a
  price shock) could produce both. If yes, the label is "both are effects of a common
  cause".

**Example 14** `Cognizant pattern`

**Statement:** Company Z says its new training programme works: attrition fell from 18% to
11% in one year. \
**Which weakens this most?** (a) The programme cost Rs.~40 lakh.
(b) In the same year the company raised salaries by 20%.
(c) Attrition in the industry is normally about 15%.

**Solution**

The gap: "attrition fell" $->$ "the training caused it".

(a) cost says nothing about cause.
(b) gives a rival cause that is well known to cut attrition, and it happened in the same
window. The fall may be entirely the pay rise. This attacks the gap directly.
(c) tells you 11% is good, which if anything helps the claim.

Size check: the fall is $18 - 11 = 7$ percentage points, i.e. $7 \div 18 = 0.3888 \approx 38.9$%
of the old rate. A 20% pay rise can easily explain a fall of that size.

**➜ Answer: (b).**

**Example 15** `TCS NQT pattern`

**Statement:** A survey of 1,000 office workers in Pune found that 62% skip breakfast on
working days. \
**Which is a valid inference?** (a) Most Indians skip breakfast.
(b) More than half of the workers surveyed skip breakfast on working days.
(c) Skipping breakfast harms health.

**Solution**

(a) jumps from 1,000 office workers in one city to a whole country. The sample does not
cover farmers, students, or other cities. Over-reach.
(b) $62% > 50%$, and it stays inside the surveyed group. Safe.
(c) brings in outside medical knowledge. The statement says nothing about health.

**➜ Answer: (b).**

---
💡 **SHORTCUT**

**The scope ruler.** For any inference, underline the group in the statement
("1,000 office workers in Pune") and the group in the option ("Indians").
If the option's group is **bigger**, or the option's time period is **longer**,
reject it without reading further. This single check settles most inference questions
in under 20 seconds.

**Example 16** `Accenture pattern`

**Statement:** A college has raised the hostel fee by 10% in order to improve the quality
of hostel food. \
**Assumptions:** I. Better food costs more money.   II. Students will pay the raised fee.

**Solution**

I — negate: "Better food does not cost more money."
Then raising the fee is not needed to improve food, and the stated reason falls apart.
Collapses. I is an assumption.

II — negate: "Students will not pay the raised fee."
Then no extra money arrives, and the food cannot be improved.
The plan cannot work. Collapses. II is an assumption.

**➜ Answer: Both I and II are assumptions.**

**Example 17** `Infosys pattern`

**Statement:** Of 500 candidates who appeared, 320 cleared the aptitude round, and 96 of
those 320 cleared the interview. \
**Conclusions:** I. 180 candidates did not clear the aptitude round.
  II. 30% of those who cleared aptitude also cleared the interview.

**Solution**

I: appeared $= 500$, cleared $= 320$.
Not cleared $= 500 - 320 = 180$. I follows.

II: interview clears $= 96$ out of $320$.
$96 \div 320 = 0.3$.
$0.3 \times 100 = 30$%. II follows.

**➜ Answer: Both I and II follow.**

---
⚠️ **TRAP**

**Percentage of what?** In Example 17, $96 \div 500 = 19.2$%, and $96 \div 320 = 30$%.
Both are true sentences about different bases. A conclusion is right only if it names the
**same** base the question used. Always read the words after "of".

**Example 18** `Wipro pattern`

**Statement:** Sector 12 floods every monsoon because its storm drains choke with plastic
waste. \
**Courses of action:** I. The drains should be cleared before the monsoon.
  II. The sale of thin plastic bags in the area should be banned.
  III. Residents of Sector 12 should be shifted elsewhere.

**Solution**

I: removes the blockage that is named as the reason. Cheap, practical, within the
municipality's power. Follows.

II: cuts the **supply** of the material that chokes the drains. It attacks the cause at the
source and is a normal municipal power. Follows.

III: moving an entire population to avoid clearing drains is extreme and costs enormously
more than the two fixes already on the table. Does not follow.

**➜ Answer: I and II follow.**

**Example 19** `Capgemini pattern`

**Statement:** An online grocer says its same-day delivery lifted repeat orders. Evidence:
in the cities that have same-day delivery, the repeat-order rate is 1.4 times the rate in
the other cities. \
**Which strengthens the claim most?** (a) Same-day delivery is popular with customers.
(b) Before same-day delivery started, those cities had the same repeat-order rate as the rest.
(c) The grocer plans to expand same-day delivery.

**Solution**

The gap: the two sets of cities may simply have been different all along.

(a) restates the claim in softer words. It adds no new fact.
(b) removes exactly the rival explanation: the cities started level, so the 1.4 times gap
opened **after** the change. Strongest.
(c) is about the future. It is not evidence for the past.

**➜ Answer: (b).**

## Tier 2 — Singapore & Thailand

### Tier 2 — Singapore & Thailand · Grab, Shopee, GIC, DBS, Agoda, SCB

**Example 20** `Grab · pattern`

**Statement:** A ride-hailing firm raised surge pricing during rain in Jakarta. Completed
trips during rain went from 6,200 per hour to 7,400 per hour. The firm concludes that
surge pricing brings more drivers online and so serves riders better. \
**Which weakens the conclusion most?** \
(a) Driver earnings per trip rose by 22%. \
(b) Ride requests during rain went from 9,000 per hour to 14,000 per hour. \
(c) Some riders complained about the higher fare.

**Solution**

The claim is "riders are served better". The right measure is the share of requests that
actually got a ride, not the raw count of trips.

Before: $"6,200" \div "9,000" = 0.6888 \dots$
$0.6888 \times 100 \approx 68.9$%.

After: $"7,400" \div "14,000" = 0.5285 \dots$
$0.5285 \times 100 \approx 52.9$%.

So the fulfilment rate fell by about $68.9 - 52.9 = 16$ percentage points.
More trips were completed, but a **smaller** share of riders got a car.

(a) supports the firm's story about drivers, not against it.
(c) is a complaint, not a measure of service.

**➜ Answer: (b) — the share of requests served fell from about 68.9% to about 52.9%.**

---
💡 **SHORTCUT**

**Raw count up does not mean rate up.** Whenever a claim rests on a count
(trips, defects, returns, accidents), hunt for the denominator
(requests, units made, orders, kilometres driven). If the denominator grew faster than
the count, the rate moved the other way. This single move answers a large share of
Tier 2 weaken questions.

**Example 21** `Shopee · pattern`

**Statement:** A seller's record: Quarter 1 — 12,000 orders and 960 returns.
Quarter 2 — 15,000 orders and 1,050 returns. \
**Conclusions:** I. The return rate fell.   II. The number of returns fell.

**Solution**

Return rate in Q1: $960 \div "12,000" = 0.08$, so $8$%.
Return rate in Q2: $"1,050" \div "15,000" = 0.07$, so $7$%.
$8% > 7%$, so the rate fell. I follows.

Number of returns: $960$ in Q1 and $"1,050"$ in Q2.
$"1,050" > 960$, so the count **rose** by $"1,050" - 960 = 90$. II does not follow.

**➜ Answer: Only I follows.**

**Example 22** `DBS · pattern`

**Statement:** A bank announces that from next month any transfer above S$10,000 to a
new payee will be held for 12 hours. The bank says this will cut scam losses. \
**Which are assumptions?** \
I. A meaningful share of scam losses comes from transfers to payees the customer has
never paid before. \
II. Victims can notice and report a scam within 12 hours of sending. \
III. No genuine customer ever needs to send a large amount urgently.

**Solution**

I — negate: "Almost no scam loss involves a new payee."
Then the hold catches nothing and the measure cannot cut losses. Collapses. Assumption.

II — negate: "Victims cannot notice within 12 hours."
Then the money is released before anyone reports it, and the hold saves nothing.
Collapses. Assumption.

III — negate: "Some genuine customers do need urgent large transfers."
The bank can still run the policy and still cut scam losses; it just inconveniences those
customers. The argument survives. Not an assumption — it is far stronger than the
argument needs.

**➜ Answer: I and II are assumptions; III is not.**

**Example 23** `Agoda · pattern`

**Statement:** Mobile bookings rose 35% in the month after the app redesign shipped. The
product team credits the redesign. \
**Which most weakens the credit given to the redesign?** \
(a) Desktop bookings were flat in the same month. \
(b) The redesign shipped in the same week a three-day national holiday sale began. \
(c) The redesign took four months to build.

**Solution**

Gap: "bookings rose after X" $->$ "X caused the rise".
The way to attack it is a rival cause that sits in the same window.

(a) actually helps the redesign story, because the redesign was mobile-only and only
mobile moved.
(b) is a rival cause with the right timing and the right size: a national holiday sale
lifts bookings on its own, redesign or not.
(c) is about effort spent. Effort is not evidence of effect.

**➜ Answer: (b).**

**Example 24** `SCB · pattern`

**Statement:** A bank's Bangkok call centre has an average wait of 11 minutes. An audit
finds that 40% of all calls are simple balance enquiries. \
**Courses of action:** I. Offer balance enquiries through an automated self-service menu.
  II. Double the number of agents.   III. Stop taking calls after 6 p.m.

**Solution**

I: the audit names a cause — 4 calls in every 10 need no human at all.
Moving them to self-service removes about 40% of the load at low cost.
Hits the cause, practical, inside the bank's power. Follows.

II: doubling agents would cut the wait, but it doubles the largest cost line to handle
work that a menu can absorb for almost nothing. With the cheaper fix named in the same
statement, this is a weak action.

III: refusing calls does not shorten the wait for the calls you do take; it pushes
customers into the next day's queue. Does not follow.

**➜ Answer: Only I clearly follows. II is weak (costly, ignores the named cause); III does not follow.**

**Example 25** `Sea · pattern`

**Statement:** In a survey of 2,400 online shoppers in Singapore, 1,560 named free returns
as the top reason they choose a platform. The company concludes: "So we must offer free
returns." \
**Part (a):** Which is a safe inference? **Part (b):** What does the conclusion assume?

**Solution**

(a) Compute the share: $"1,560" \div "2,400" = 0.65$, so $65$%.
Safe inference: 65% of the shoppers surveyed named free returns as their top reason.
It stays inside the surveyed group and adds no new claim.

Unsafe: "65% of all Singaporeans want free returns" — the survey covered online shoppers,
not everyone.

(b) The jump is from **these 2,400 shoppers** to **our customers**.
Assumption: the company's own target customers behave like the surveyed group.
Negate it — if the company's customers are nothing like this sample, the survey gives no
reason to change anything, and the conclusion falls.

**➜ Answer: (a) 65% of the surveyed shoppers named free returns. (b) That the company's target
customers resemble the surveyed group.**

**Example 26** `LINE MAN · pattern`

**Statement:** A delivery firm pilots "batching" — two orders carried on one rider trip.
Over two weeks the cost per order fell from THB~38 to THB~33. The firm says batching cuts
cost. \
**Which strengthens, and which weakens?** \
I. Average delivery time and cancellation rate were unchanged over the two weeks. \
II. Rider pay per trip was cut by 15% at the start of the same two weeks.

**Solution**

Size of the effect: fall $= 38 - 33 = 5$ baht.
As a share: $5 \div 38 = 0.1315 \dots$, so about $13.2$%.

I strengthens. The obvious counter to batching is "you saved money by making service
worse". I shuts that door: the same speed, the same cancellations, lower cost.

II weakens. A 15% cut in rider pay lowers cost per order all by itself, with no batching
at all. It is a rival cause of the right size to explain a 13.2% fall.

**➜ Answer: I strengthens; II weakens.**

**Example 27** `GIC · pattern`

**Question:** Should a pension fund move 10% of its equity holding into infrastructure debt? \
**Arguments:** I. Yes — over the last ten years infrastructure debt returned about 8.2% a
year with smaller year-to-year swings than equity, and the fund pays out over a 20-year
horizon.   II. No — infrastructure projects can be delayed.

**Solution**

I is strong. It gives a number (8.2%), a comparison on the thing that matters to a
pension fund (swings versus payout horizon), and it is tied to this exact decision.

II is weak **as written**. Every asset carries risk; the argument gives no size, no
frequency, and no comparison with the risk of the equity it would replace.

**Example 29** `Google · pattern`

**Statement:** A study of 300 successful startups found that 78% of them had a "fail fast"
culture. The study concludes that a fail-fast culture drives success. \
**Name the flaw, and show with numbers how the conclusion could be exactly backwards.**

**Solution**

*Insight: the sample was chosen by the outcome. A rate measured only among winners tells
you nothing until you know the same rate among losers.*

The number 78% answers: "among successes, how many had the culture?"
The conclusion needs the reverse: "among firms with the culture, how many succeeded?"

Build a world where the study's fact is true and the conclusion is false.
Suppose the period had 300 successes and 5,000 failures.

Successes with the culture $= 0.78 \times 300 = 234$.
Suppose 80% of the failures also had it: $0.80 \times "5,000" = "4,000"$.

Now compute success rate **by culture**.
With the culture: total $= 234 + "4,000" = "4,234"$; successes $= 234$.
Rate $= 234 \div "4,234" = 0.0552 \dots \approx 5.5$%.

Without the culture: successes $= 300 - 234 = 66$; failures $= "5,000" - "4,000" = "1,000"$;
total $= "1,066"$.
Rate $= 66 \div "1,066" = 0.0619 \dots \approx 6.2$%.

So in this world the fail-fast firms succeed **less** often ($5.5% < 6.2$%), while the
study's 78% figure is still perfectly true.

**➜ Answer: Survivorship bias. The study needs the culture rate among failed startups too;
without it the 78% figure is consistent with the culture making success less likely.**

**Example 30** `Amazon · pattern`

**Statement:** A fraud filter flags 1 transaction in every 50. It catches 95% of all
fraudulent transactions. Fraud is 0.4% of all transactions. A manager says: "Since it
catches 95% of fraud, a flagged transaction is almost certainly fraud." \
**Is the manager right? Compute.**

**Solution**

*Insight: "catches 95% of fraud" is about the fraud column. "A flagged item is fraud" is
about the flagged row. With a tiny base rate, those two numbers are miles apart.*

Work with 100,000 transactions.

Fraudulent transactions $= 0.4% \times "100,000" = 0.004 \times "100,000" = 400$.
Caught by the filter $= 95% \times 400 = 0.95 \times 400 = 380$.

Total flagged $= "100,000" \div 50 = "2,000"$.

Of those 2,000 flags, only 380 are actually fraud.
Share $= 380 \div "2,000" = 0.19$, so $19$%.

The other $"2,000" - 380 = "1,620"$ flags are clean transactions — that is $81$% of all flags.

So a flagged transaction is fraud about 1 time in 5, not "almost certainly".

**➜ Answer: No. Only about 19% of flagged transactions are fraud; 81% are false alarms.**

---
💡 **SHORTCUT**

**The 100,000 table.** For any "the test caught X% so a positive means Y" argument, write
four numbers for 100,000 cases: true cases, caught cases, total positives, and positives
that are false. You never need probability notation. Two multiplications and one division
settle it, and it works for fraud filters, medical tests, spam filters and
security alerts alike.

**Example 31** `Goldman Sachs · pattern`

**Statement:** A trading desk put its 10 worst-performing analysts into a coaching
programme. The next quarter, that group's average profit improved by 30%, while the top
10 analysts, who got no coaching, slipped by 8%. The head of desk concludes that coaching
works and should be extended. \
**What is wrong, and what evidence would settle it?**

**Solution**

*Insight: the two groups were picked by their extreme results in one quarter. Extreme
results contain luck, and luck does not repeat. Both groups would drift toward the middle
next quarter with no coaching at all.*

Look at what the data itself shows. The top 10 got **no** coaching and still moved — and
they moved **downward**, toward the middle. The bottom 10 moved upward, toward the middle.
Both groups moved in the direction the "drift to the average" story predicts, and only
one of them was coached.

So the desk's own control group already behaves as if the effect is drift, not coaching.
The 30% figure is measured against a quarter that was unusually bad, which inflates it.

What would settle it: take the bottom 20 analysts, and assign 10 at random to coaching
and 10 at random to none. Both groups then drift equally, so any extra gain in the
coached half is the coaching. Random assignment **inside** the extreme group is the key —
comparing an extreme group with a non-extreme group cannot work.

**➜ Answer: Regression to the average. The fix is a randomised control group drawn from the same
bottom-10 pool.**

**Example 32** `Microsoft · pattern`

**Argument:** "Our app now loads in under one second. So users will not abandon it." \
**Sort these into necessary assumption, sufficient assumption, and neither:** \
(a) Load time is one reason users abandon apps. \
(b) Any app that loads in under one second is never abandoned. \
(c) Before the change, the app took three seconds to load. \
(d) No competitor's app loads in under one second.

**Solution**

*Insight: a necessary assumption is the smallest thing the argument cannot live without.
A sufficient assumption is a bigger claim that, if handed to you free, finishes the
argument. Tests differ: negate for necessary, add for sufficient.*

(a) Negation test: "Load time is **not** a reason users abandon apps."
Then making the app fast cannot stop abandonment, and the conclusion dies.
The argument cannot live without (a). **Necessary.**

(b) Negation test: "Some app that loads under one second is abandoned."
Does the argument die? Not necessarily — our app could still be one of the ones that is
not abandoned. So (b) is not necessary.
Addition test: if (b) is handed to us as true, then our sub-second app is never abandoned,
and the conclusion follows with no gap left. **Sufficient, not necessary.**

(c) Negation: "Before the change, the app did not take three seconds to load."
Say it took 1.5 seconds before. The argument still reads the same: it is now under one
second, so users will not abandon it. The argument survives, so (c) is not necessary.
Addition test: knowing the old load time still leaves the gap open — nothing links load
time to abandonment. **Neither.**

(d) Negation: "Some competitor also loads under one second."
The conclusion is about **abandonment**, not about being fastest. The argument survives.
Adding (d) also does not close the gap. **Neither.**

**➜ Answer: (a) necessary; (b) sufficient; (c) and (d) neither.**

**Example 33** `Uber · pattern`

**Statement:** A city capped the number of ride-hailing licences. Average driver earnings
then rose from Rs.~1,150 to Rs.~1,380 per day. The regulator says the cap made drivers
better off. \
*Build a numeric case in which the figures are exactly right and yet not one driver
earned a single rupee more.*

**Solution**

*Insight: an average is computed over whoever is still in the group. Remove the weakest
members and the average jumps, even if every individual is frozen.*

Take 100 drivers before the cap, and split them into two groups.
Let 75 drivers earn Rs.~1,380 a day, and 25 drivers earn Rs.~460 a day.

Check the before-average.
Earnings of the 75 $= 75 \times "1,380" = "103,500"$.
Earnings of the 25 $= 25 \times 460 = "11,500"$.
Total $= "103,500" + "11,500" = "115,000"$.
Average $= "115,000" \div 100 = "1,150"$. That matches the "before" figure exactly.

Now apply the cap. Licences are cut, and the 25 lowest earners — the part-timers with the
fewest trips — lose theirs and leave the market.

After: 75 drivers remain, each still earning Rs.~1,380 a day, exactly as before.
Total $= 75 \times "1,380" = "103,500"$.
Average $= "103,500" \div 75 = "1,380"$. That matches the "after" figure exactly.

Not one remaining driver earned more than before, and 25 drivers lost their livelihood
entirely. The average rose only because the group changed.

To settle it properly the regulator must track the **same** drivers before and after, not
the average of whoever holds a licence at the time.

**➜ Answer: Selection effect. Dropping the 25 lowest earners raises the average from 1,150 to
1,380 with every surviving driver's pay unchanged.**

---
⚠️ **TRAP**

**Ratios and averages both hide a changing denominator.** "Cost per order fell",
"average GPA rose", "defects per unit dropped", "earnings per driver rose" — before you
accept any of them as an improvement, ask two questions:
**did the top line change, or did the bottom line change?** and
**is the group being measured the same group as before?**
An average that rises because the weakest left is not an improvement.

**Example 34** `Adobe · pattern`

**Statement:** A design tool's team reports that average time to first export fell from 22
minutes to 14 minutes after a redesign. They conclude designers are more productive. \
**Part (a):** Name the gap. **Part (b):** Show numerically how a single extra fact could
shrink the gain to almost nothing. **Part (c):** What fact would make the claim solid?

**Solution**

*Insight: the team measured a milestone, not the job. A milestone can move earlier
without the job finishing any sooner.*

(a) The gap is between "time to first export" and "productivity".
Productivity is output per unit of total time. First export is one checkpoint inside the
work, and a redesign can move that checkpoint without changing the finish line.

(b) Headline gain: $22 - 14 = 8$ minutes, i.e. $8 \div 22 = 0.3636 \dots \approx 36.4$%.

Now add this fact: in the new tool, users export a rough draft early and then spend an
average of 6 more minutes editing **after** that first export.

True time to a finished asset, new tool $= 14 + 6 = 20$ minutes.
Gain against the old 22 minutes $= 22 - 20 = 2$ minutes.
As a share: $2 \div 22 = 0.0909 \dots \approx 9.1$%.

So a headline "36.4% faster" becomes a real "9.1% faster" — and if the post-export edit
were 8 minutes or more, the gain would vanish completely.

(c) The claim needs the end-to-end number: time from starting a project to delivering the
finished asset, measured on the same kind of projects. For example, "median project
completion fell from 3.2 hours to 2.1 hours" — a fall of $1.1 \div 3.2 = 0.34375 \approx 34.4$%
on the measure that actually matters.

**➜ Answer: (a) milestone is not the same as total output; (b) with 6 minutes of post-export
editing the real gain drops from 36.4% to 9.1%; (c) end-to-end project completion time.**

**Example 35** `D. E. Shaw · pattern`

**Statement:** At an exchange, the retail segment's volume grew 40% in 2024 while the
institutional segment grew 10%. Base volumes at the start of 2024 were: retail 120 units,
institutional 900 units. An internal note says: "Retail is now adding more new volume than
institutional." \
**Part (a):** Is the note right? **Part (b):** Below what institutional base would it be right?

**Solution**

*Insight: a growth rate is a ratio; new volume is a rate times a base. Comparing rates
answers a different question from comparing increases.*

(a) Retail increase $= 40% \times 120 = 0.40 \times 120 = 48$ units.
Institutional increase $= 10% \times 900 = 0.10 \times 900 = 90$ units.

$90 > 48$. Institutional added 90 units of new volume against retail's 48 — almost double.
The note is wrong.

Check the totals to be sure.
Retail end $= 120 + 48 = 168$. Institutional end $= 900 + 90 = 990$.
Total start $= 120 + 900 = "1,020"$. Total end $= 168 + 990 = "1,158"$.
Total increase $= "1,158" - "1,020" = 138$, and $48 + 90 = 138$. Consistent.

Note also the share of total growth: retail contributed $48 \div 138 = 0.3478 \approx 34.8$%,
institutional $90 \div 138 \approx 65.2$%.

(b) Let the institutional base be $I$, with the same rates.
Retail adds $0.40 \times 120 = 48$.
Institutional adds $0.10 \times I$.

Retail adds more when $48 > 0.10 I$.
Divide both sides by $0.10$: $480 > I$.

So retail would add more only if the institutional base were below 480 units.
The actual base, 900 units, is nearly twice that.

**➜ Answer: (a) No — institutional added 90 units against retail's 48.
(b) Only if the institutional base were below 480 units.**

+ **Only I is strong.** It cites the company's own long-run data on both output and cost. "Employees may feel lonely" is unmeasured and offers no comparison with the gains.
+ **Does not follow.** Almost all driving is done near home, so most accidents will be near home even if every kilometre is equally risky. The claim needs accidents **per kilometre driven** at each distance, not the share of accidents.
+ **Only I.** A patched build fixes the named cause for the 14% affected, at ordinary engineering cost and fully within the bank's control. Telling customers to buy new phones is outside the bank's power and would lose those users.

## 📋 One-page revision card

**THE THREE PARTS** — Evidence $->$ **gap** $->$ Conclusion. Every question attacks the gap.
Conclusion markers: so, hence, therefore, thus, this shows, we must.

**THE SEVEN TYPES AND THEIR TESTS**
#table(columns: (auto, 1fr),
  [Assumption], [Negate it. Argument collapses $\\Rightarrow$ it is an assumption.],
  [Conclusion], [Derivable from the given words alone? No outside facts.],
  [Inference],  [Stays inside the stated group and period.],
  [Argument],   [Strong $=$ relevant $+$ sized $+$ tied to this exact question.],
  [Course of action], [Hits the cause? Practical? Within the actor's power?],
  [Cause–effect], [Which came first and could stand alone?],
  [Strengthen / weaken], [Close the gap, or supply a rival cause.],
)

**ASSUMPTION vs INFERENCE** — assumption sits **before** the statement and is unsaid;
inference sits **after** it and is drawn out.

**SHORTCUTS**
+ **Scope ruler** — if the option's group or time period is bigger than the statement's,
  reject the inference at once.
+ **Raw count vs rate** — for any count claim, find the denominator. If the denominator
  grew faster, the rate moved the other way.
+ **The 100,000 table** — for any "test catches X%" claim, write: true cases, caught cases,
  total positives, false positives. Two multiplications and one division.
+ **Rate $\times$ base $=$ increase** — a 40% rise on 120 adds 48; a 10% rise on 900 adds 90.
+ **Necessary vs sufficient** — negate for necessary, add for sufficient.

**TOP 5 TRAPS**
+ **Extreme words.** "All", "never", "only", "no one" almost always break an assumption.
  An assumption is the **minimum** the argument needs.
+ **Percentage of what?** $96 \div 500 = 19.2$% and $96 \div 320 = 30$% are both true. Check the base.
+ **True but weak.** A true sentence is a weak argument if it has no size or applies
  equally to every option.
+ **After therefore because.** "B followed A" is not "A caused B". Hunt for the rival cause
  sitting in the same window.
+ **The group changed.** An average rises when the weakest leave. A rate falls when the
  denominator grows. Ask whether the thing measured is still the same thing.
